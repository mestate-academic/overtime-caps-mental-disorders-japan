# Statutory overtime caps and compensated mental disorders in Japan: two natural experiments in 10 years of national data, 2016–2025

## ABSTRACT

**Objectives** Japan capped overtime for most industries from April 2019/2020 and for construction and motor-vehicle driving, previously exempt, from April 2024, creating two natural experiments. We examined whether either cap was followed by fewer compensated work-related mental disorders.

**Methods** Using national workers' compensation data for fiscal years (FY) 2016–2025, we compared approvals and claims for mental disorders per million employed persons between construction and transport and six comparison industries, using Poisson difference-in-differences and event-study models with wild cluster bootstrap inference. Experiment 1 compared FY2016–2018 with FY2019–2023; experiment 2 compared FY2021–2023 with FY2024–2025. Cerebro-cardiovascular disease, recognised mainly on the basis of long hours, served as a positive control for exposure. Sensitivity, placebo and prefecture-level analyses were added.

**Results** Approvals more than doubled over the decade (498 to 1082). Neither cap was associated with a clear relative decline in the newly capped industries (experiment 1: incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.07, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.50; experiment 2: 1.10, 0.93 to 1.50). Approvals in construction and transport rose in FY2023, before the cap, making experiment 2 sensitive to that year. Cerebro-cardiovascular approvals did not decline, but estimates were imprecise. Approvals classified under harassment rose from 21% to 50%, and those attributed to working hours fell from 17% to 6%.

**Conclusions** Approvals for work-related mental disorders, counted by year of decision, were not lower in industries newly covered by statutory overtime caps. Because actual hours, compliance and timing of onset were not measured, this does not show that reducing actual working hours is ineffective.

## KEY MESSAGES

**What is already known on this topic**
- Long working hours have been linked to cardiovascular deaths and depressive symptoms, but whether statutory overtime caps reduce compensated work-related mental disorders is unknown.

**What this study adds**
- In two natural experiments within 10 years of Japanese national data, neither overtime cap was associated with a clearly detectable relative reduction in compensated work-related mental disorders.
- Cerebro-cardiovascular disease, the outcome most directly tied to long hours, also did not decline; the doubling of approvals was concentrated in cases classified under harassment.

**How this study might affect research, practice or policy**
- Compensation statistics alone cannot show whether working-time policy works; monitoring should include measured hours, including unrecorded overtime.
- Prevention should also target harassment by supervisors and customers; these results should not be read as showing that the caps are unnecessary.

## INTRODUCTION

The WHO/ILO Joint Estimates attributed 745 000 deaths from stroke and ischaemic heart disease in 2016 to working 55 hours or more per week.¹ For depression, a WHO/ILO systematic review judged the evidence inadequate,² although cohort studies have found an association with depressive symptoms, particularly in Asia.³ Japan had no binding legal limit on overtime until the Work Style Reform Act of 2018.⁴ The Act capped overtime at 45 hours a month and 360 hours a year (up to 720 hours a year and 100 hours a month in exceptional cases), with penalties for violations. It applied to large firms from April 2019 and to small and medium-sized firms from April 2020; construction, motor-vehicle driving and physicians were exempt until April 2024 (the "2024 problem").⁵

The caps reduced paid overtime by about 25%⁶ and shortened long weekly hours⁷ and, among workers most likely to work long hours, reduced overtime above 45 and 80 hours a month,⁸ but some men replaced paid with unpaid overtime and gained no well-being.⁶ Earlier, a 2010 increase in the overtime wage premium reduced working hours and improved mental health,⁹ and the 2014 Act on overwork prevention, which set no limit on hours, was followed by an estimated 26% fall in compensated cerebro-cardiovascular disease, about 41% of it mediated by shorter hours; mental disorders were not examined.¹⁰ Evaluations of the caps, including the government's own,¹¹ have measured hours, wages and self-reported well-being; whether binding caps reduced work-related mental disorders is unknown. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) publishes annual claims and approvals for mental disorders by industry, prefecture and precipitating event;¹² approvals have risen every year since FY2018, exceeding 1000 in FY2024,¹² and rates have long been among the highest in transport and postal services.¹³

The two-step introduction created two natural experiments within one decade (figure 1): from April 2019/2020, six industries were capped while construction and transport were not; from April 2024, construction and transport were capped while the six already were. Because compensation data also respond to recognition criteria—revised in September 2023 to list customer harassment as a precipitating event¹⁴—and to claiming behaviour, we also analysed cerebro-cardiovascular disease, for which long hours are the main recognition criterion, as a positive control for exposure. We asked whether either cap was followed by fewer compensated mental disorders in the newly capped industries.

## METHODS

### Design and data
We conducted a quasi-experimental study using publicly available aggregate data for FY2016–2025 (April 2016 to March 2026), reported according to STROBE.¹⁵ From the MHLW annual reports¹² (FY2025 edition published July 2026) we transcribed, by industry, claims and approvals for mental disorders and cerebro-cardiovascular diseases, and national approvals by precipitating event. Overlapping years in successive reports were cross-checked, approvals were checked against a ministry compilation,¹⁶ and all transcribed tables summed to the published national totals (online supplemental table S1). Prefecture counts are published from FY2021. Counts refer to the fiscal year in which claims were filed or decided, not the year of onset. Denominators were annual average employed persons by industry from the Labour Force Survey¹⁷ and, for prefectures, the 2015 Population Census;¹⁸ a sensitivity analysis used employees, excluding the self-employed and family workers not covered by compensation insurance.¹⁷

### Two natural experiments
The exempt group comprised construction and transport and postal services (which includes motor-vehicle driving); the comparison group comprised the six other industries reported separately throughout with consistent denominators: manufacturing; wholesale and retail; finance and insurance; education; information and communication; and accommodation and food services. Both groups contain workers whose coverage did not change at the cap (for example, office staff in construction), so the estimand is the association between an industry's legal coverage and its compensation rates, not the effect on individual covered workers. Health care and welfare, capped in two steps (physicians, about 4% of its workers, only from April 2024), fits neither group and is shown for reference only. Because each cap took effect on 1 April, every fiscal year lies wholly before or after each cap.

- **Experiment 1 (April 2019/2020 cap):** the six comparison industries were newly capped (large firms from April 2019, small and medium-sized firms from April 2020; FY2019 was a transition year) and construction and transport served as controls; FY2016–2018 was compared with FY2019–2023.
- **Experiment 2 (April 2024 cap):** construction and transport were newly capped and the six industries, capped since 2019/2020, served as controls; FY2021–2023 was compared with FY2024–2025.

The experiments were estimated separately to avoid problematic comparisons arising from staggered treatment timing in a pooled model.¹⁹ Because its controls were already capped, experiment 2 estimates the change in construction and transport relative to already-capped industries, not the effect against an uncapped counterfactual; any continuing change in the effect of the earlier cap among the controls would enter this estimate.

### Statistical analysis
We fitted Poisson regressions of annual counts with industry and year fixed effects, an interaction between the newly capped group and the post-cap period, and log employed persons as offset. The incidence rate ratio (IRR) is the ratio of the after/before rate ratio in the newly capped group to that in the control group; it assumes parallel trends without the cap, which we examined with event studies interacting the newly capped group with each year, relative to the mean (log scale) of each experiment's pre-cap period (experiment 2: window from FY2020, when all controls were capped; reference FY2021–2023). The primary outcome was approved claims for mental disorders; secondary outcomes were claims for mental disorders and approvals and claims for cerebro-cardiovascular diseases. The latter served as a positive control for exposure: long hours are their main recognition criterion, and industry-average hours predict compensated cases,²⁰ so they should decline if the caps reduced extreme hours, whereas changes in claiming common to both diseases would move them together.

Each experiment includes only eight industries, and heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors ignore serial correlation within industries. The primary method of inference was therefore a wild cluster score bootstrap clustered by industry with Webb weights (9999 draws; 95% CIs by test inversion).²¹⁻²³ Because only two industries were newly capped in experiment 2, and only two served as controls in experiment 1, even this may be unreliable.²⁴ As sensitivity analyses we report industry-clustered standard errors with t(7) critical values, a placebo permutation analysis re-estimating the model with each of the 28 pairs of industries as placebo-treated (descriptive, because the caps were not randomly assigned), and estimates from the panel collapsed to one pre-cap and one post-cap period.²⁵ Prespecified sensitivity analyses excluded FY2023 (anticipation), FY2020–2021 (COVID-19) and accommodation and food services, extended experiment 2's pre-period to FY2016, and analysed construction and transport separately. Post hoc analyses excluded the FY2019 transition year, varied experiment 2's pre-period, added health care and welfare to the newly capped group, excluded the comparison industries most exposed to customer harassment (wholesale and retail; accommodation and food services) and used employees as the denominator. A prefecture panel (47 prefectures, FY2021–2025) related the 2015 share of employment in construction and transport (standardised) to approvals after 2024, with prefecture and year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by prefecture. Approvals by precipitating event were grouped into harassment, change in workload, working hours (≥80 hours of overtime in a month or ≥2 weeks of continuous work), accidents and injury, special events and other. Analyses used Python 3 and statsmodels 0.15; data and code are available at https://github.com/mestate-academic/overtime-caps-mental-disorders-japan.

### Patient and public involvement
Patients and the public were not involved.

### Ethics
Only publicly available aggregate statistics without information on identifiable individuals were used. Such research falls outside the scope of the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects,²⁶ and ethical review was not required.

## RESULTS

### Ten-year trends
Over the 10 years, approvals for mental disorders more than doubled, from 498 in FY2016 to 1082 in FY2025, and rose in every industry group (figure 1; table 1). The approval rate rose from 7.2 to 12.6 per million employed persons in the six comparison industries, from 11.9 to 24.5 in construction and transport, and from 9.9 to 30.8 in health care and welfare. Claims rose faster (24.0 to 64.2, 33.9 to 80.8 and 37.4 to 136.0 per million). Cerebro-cardiovascular approvals did not rise (table 1).

### Experiment 1: the April 2019/2020 cap
Approvals in the newly capped industries did not decline relative to construction and transport (IRR 1.07, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.50; table 2), and claims did not change significantly (1.12, 0.82 to 1.53). Cerebro-cardiovascular approvals (1.01, 0.40 to 1.57) and claims (0.96, 0.75 to 1.18) did not change, but these estimates were imprecise. In the event study (figure 2A), pre-cap estimates for FY2016–2018 were close to 1 (0.94 to 1.04), consistent with parallel trends; approvals in the newly capped industries were not lower than before the cap in any year from FY2019 to FY2022 (IRRs 1.06 to 1.27), and the IRR of 0.79 in FY2023 reflects the rise in construction and transport that year. Cerebro-cardiovascular approvals did not change in any year (0.93 to 1.14).

### Experiment 2: the April 2024 cap
Approvals in construction and transport did not decline relative to the six comparison industries (IRR 1.10, 0.93 to 1.50). Claims (0.94, 0.85 to 1.21) and cerebro-cardiovascular approvals (0.96, 0.59 to 1.22) and claims (0.98, 0.86 to 1.10) changed little. In the event study (figure 2B), the relative rate in construction and transport was stable in FY2020–2022 (IRRs 0.87 to 0.99), rose in FY2023, the year before the cap (1.32, 1.20 to 1.46), and did not fall below the pre-cap level in FY2024 (1.08) or FY2025 (1.18) (online supplemental table S2). The FY2023 rise coincided with the September 2023 revision of the recognition criteria and intense media coverage of the "2024 problem"; it makes the parallel-trends assumption questionable for experiment 2. With FY2022 as the reference year (analysis in the data repository), the IRRs were 1.53 in FY2023, 1.25 in FY2024 and 1.37 in FY2025. Cerebro-cardiovascular approvals did not change in any year (0.85 to 1.07).

### Robustness
No method of inference indicated a decline in approvals (online supplemental table S3b): descriptive placebo permutation p values were 0.68 and 0.36, and collapsed two-period estimates were 1.07 (0.94 to 1.21) and 1.10 (1.02 to 1.19). For some secondary outcomes, statistical significance depended on the method: for claims in experiment 2, the collapsed estimate was 0.95 (0.92 to 0.98), whereas the bootstrap interval included 1. Excluding FY2023 gave 1.19 (0.90 to 1.62) in experiment 1 and 1.30 (1.07 to 2.00) in experiment 2; because the rise preceded the cap, this shows how strongly experiment 2 depends on the reference period, not an increase caused by the cap. Other prespecified and post hoc analyses gave IRRs for approvals of 0.99 to 1.17 (online supplemental table S3a), including 0.99 and 1.10 after excluding the comparison industries most exposed to customer harassment and 1.06 and 1.10 with employees as the denominator; the non-employee share (about 12% in construction and transport, about 6% in the comparison industries) changed little. The experiment 2 estimate lay within the placebo distribution (25th of 28 placebo estimates, range 0.79 to 1.15; rank p=0.36), indicating no detectable effect rather than proof of no effect. In the prefecture panel, exposure ranged from 9.7% (Tokyo) to 16.5% (Miyagi), and the IRR per SD was 1.03 (0.95 to 1.12) for approvals and 0.99 (0.96 to 1.01) for claims (online supplemental table S4).

### Precipitating events
Over the decade, events classified as harassment rose from 21% to 50% of approvals, whereas working-hour events fell from 17% to 6% and did not increase in number (86 in FY2016, 70 in FY2025) (table 3). Of the 346 additional approvals between FY2022 and FY2024, 195 (56%) involved harassment—108 by customers, recognised from September 2023, and 77 by supervisors—and 28 (8%) working hours. Because harassment categories were added or redefined in May 2020 and September 2023, these shares are not fully comparable over time.

## DISCUSSION

### Principal findings
In two natural experiments within 10 years of national data, compensated mental disorders were not lower in the newly capped industries after the cap. Point estimates for approvals were above 1 and no method of inference indicated a decline, although intervals were wide once the small number of industries was taken into account. Recognition in construction and transport rose before the April 2024 cap, and the doubling of approvals was concentrated in cases classified under harassment rather than working hours, although changes in recognition criteria and claiming may have contributed. Cerebro-cardiovascular disease, the outcome most directly tied to long hours, did not decline after either cap.

### Were the caps complied with?
Our exposure was the legal cap, not the hours actually worked, and compliance was incomplete. In FY2024, inspectors visiting 26 512 workplaces suspected of long hours found illegal overtime in 42.4%; in these, the longest-working employee exceeded 80 hours of overtime in a month in 48.7% and 100 hours in 28.4%, and working time was inadequately recorded in 15.1% of all workplaces inspected.²⁷ These workplaces are not representative, but hours reported by households have long exceeded those reported by employers, by an estimated few hundred hours per worker per year depending on the method, a gap usually attributed to unpaid overtime,²⁸ ²⁹ and after the 2019 cap some men replaced paid with unpaid overtime.⁶ Managers, workers under discretionary schemes and physicians were partly or wholly outside the caps. On the other hand, the 2019 cap reduced overtime above 45 and 80 hours a month among workers most likely to work long hours,⁸ so extreme hours may have fallen for some high-risk workers.

If the caps reduced recorded rather than actual working hours, the change in relevant exposure may have been smaller than the legal change, potentially attenuating any observable effect on compensation outcomes. The positive control does not settle this question: cerebro-cardiovascular approvals did not fall, but the bootstrap intervals (0.40 to 1.57 and 0.59 to 1.22) included substantial declines, and recognition also depends on factors other than hours, so their failure to decline is not evidence of non-compliance. Our findings therefore concern the legal caps, not the effect of reducing actual hours. Studies that make actual working time visible—for example, by estimating unrecorded overtime by industry from household and employer surveys, or by using records of computer use and building entry—could overturn these findings.

### Other explanations
Three further mechanisms may contribute. First, working-hour events accounted for only 15–18% of approvals before the caps; most cases follow harassment, changes in workload or accidents, which the caps do not address. That hours-related approvals did not grow while total approvals doubled is compatible with a modest effect of the caps on such cases, which cannot be identified here. Second, regulation may raise awareness of compensation rights: claims tended to rise in the newly capped industries after the April 2019/2020 cap, and approvals in construction and transport rose in FY2023, amid media coverage of the "2024 problem" but before the cap applied. Third, approvals rose in all industries after the September 2023 revision of the recognition criteria,¹⁴ which may have affected them unequally, although excluding the comparison industries most exposed to customer harassment did not change the results.

### Comparison with other studies
Earlier evaluations found fewer paid overtime hours with partial substitution by unpaid overtime⁶ and better self-rated health,⁷ declining long hours in construction and logistics before 2024,⁵ and an attenuated decline in suicide among employed men around the time of the reform.³⁰ We add that these changes have not, so far, reduced compensated mental disorders. Lin et al. reported fewer compensated cerebro-cardiovascular diseases after the 2014 Act,¹⁰ whereas we found no clear additional relative reduction after the later caps; the studies are not directly comparable, because they evaluated different policies, periods and estimands (a nationwide before-after change versus relative changes between industries). In compensated cases from 2010–2014, long working hours were the most frequent event among men;³¹ the recent predominance of interpersonal events is consistent with meta-analyses linking workplace bullying to depression.³² ³³ Japan's harassment-prevention law, which required employers to prevent power harassment from June 2020 (large firms) and April 2022 (small and medium-sized firms), reduced self-reported harassment by about 7%,³⁴ but its effect on harassment-related disorders is unknown. Employers were not required to prevent customer harassment until 1 October 2026, after our study period;³⁵ FY2027 will be the first full fiscal year in which compensation data can reflect that duty.

### Strengths and limitations
Strengths are complete national data over 10 years, two natural experiments in which the roles of the groups reverse, employment denominators, a positive control for exposure, and prespecified sensitivity and placebo analyses. There are limitations. First, exposure was the legal cap, not measured hours. Second, the design is ecological with eight industries per experiment, of which only two were newly capped (experiment 2) or served as controls (experiment 1), so inference is imprecise and may remain unreliable even with the wild cluster bootstrap;²⁴ industry divisions also include many workers whose coverage never changed, and the 2019/2020 cap reached small and medium-sized firms a year after large firms, which industry data cannot separate. Third, experiment 1 overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic; excluding pandemic years or accommodation and food services did not change the results, but other industry-specific shocks cannot be excluded. Fourth, experiment 2 has only two post-cap years and already-capped controls, so it estimates a relative change between industry groups rather than the absolute effect of the 2024 cap. Counts are by year of decision, so FY2024–2025 approvals include illnesses that began before April 2024; this may attenuate an immediate effect, but the direction and size of any bias are uncertain because filing and decision delays may vary over time and between industries. Fifth, compensation data capture only claimed and recognised cases; the recognition criteria for mental disorders were revised in May 2020 and September 2023¹⁴ and those for cerebro-cardiovascular disease in September 2021,³⁶ all nationwide and therefore absorbed by year fixed effects unless their effect differed by industry; because earlier revisions in Japan and Taiwan were followed by more than twofold increases in recognised cerebro-cardiovascular cases,³⁷ ³⁸ unequal effects could bias our estimates. Sixth, prefecture-level exposure varied little (SD 1.3 percentage points). Seventh, the main denominators included the self-employed, who are not covered by compensation insurance; this understates rates, but the non-employee share was stable and employees as the denominator gave almost identical estimates. Denominators were not adjusted for age or sex.

### Implications
Preventing work-related mental disorders in Japan requires action on psychosocial hazards—above all harassment by supervisors and customers—alongside working-time regulation, and enforcement should target the recording of actual hours. Compensation statistics, whose annual records are widely reported, should not be used alone to judge working-time policy. This matters now: in October 2025, as the five-year review of the Act began, the government instructed the ministry to consider relaxing working-time regulation,³⁹ and from 1 September 2026 labour inspectors stopped uniformly requiring firms to reduce overtime to 45 hours a month, although the statutory caps are unchanged.⁴⁰ Our results do not show that the caps are unnecessary: they concern compensation outcomes under incomplete compliance, not the health effects of reducing actual hours. Linking compensation records to measured hours, and longer follow-up, would allow a more definitive evaluation.

## CONCLUSION
In national workers' compensation data, the introduction of Japan's statutory overtime caps was not associated with a clear relative reduction in approvals for work-related mental disorders in the industries studied, and the doubling of approvals was concentrated in cases classified under harassment. Industry-level differences in coverage, changes in recognition criteria, delayed decisions and the absence of measured working hours limit causal interpretation. These findings should not be read as evidence that reducing actual working hours is ineffective in preventing mental disorders.
